# The Window Effect

**A first semester physics project on why an object looks smaller as you walk toward it.**

Why does the Taj Mahal look smaller as you walk closer to it, instead of bigger? I first noticed the same thing from my apartment window in Karachi, where a school was visible. Walk toward the window and the school seemed to shrink. Step back and it grew.

Most people say it is just the architecture. I was not convinced. Even illusions have logic. So I built a small model, wrote my own theory, and worked out the math. I call it the **Window Effect**.

---

## The idea in one line

The window is a fixed frame. Stand close and you see a wide slice of the object crammed into that small frame, so it looks small. Stand back and a narrow slice fills the same frame, so it looks big.

This is the same physics as a camera zoom. A narrow angle of view frames less of the scene, so things look bigger and closer.

## What I measured

I placed a 3 cm x 3 cm window in front of an object, kept the object 5 cm behind the window, and took readings from three distances.

| Distance from window | Angle of view | Width of object seen |
|----------------------|---------------|----------------------|
| 2 cm (closest)       | 73 degrees    | 10.5 cm              |
| 5 cm                 | 34 degrees    | 6 cm                 |
| 7 cm (farthest)      | 25 degrees    | 5.2 cm               |

Closer to the window, the angle of view is wide and a large slice of the object is squeezed into the frame, so it looks small. Farther away, the angle is narrow and a small slice fills the frame, so it looks big.

## Theory vs experiment

I did not stop at the readings. I calculated the same angles and widths from geometry (trigonometry and the Pythagoras theorem), and the calculated values matched the measured ones almost exactly. That match is the part I am most proud of.

![Angle of view against distance](images/graph.jpg)

The graph shows that as distance from the window increases, the angle of view decreases. The two are inversely related.

## A note on originality

After finishing, I found that this belongs to a known family of illusions (aperture and framing effects, related to how perceived size works). I did not know that when I started. I reasoned it out on my own, and my math lined up with the established science. I am sharing it as a student project, not as a new discovery.
